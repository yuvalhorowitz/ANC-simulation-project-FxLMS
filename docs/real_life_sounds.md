# Real Life Sounds - ANC Performance Analysis

## Overview

This document summarizes our findings from testing the FxLMS-based ANC system with real car noise recordings, as opposed to the synthetic noise scenarios (highway, city, idle, acceleration) generated by the NoiseMixer.

## Real Audio Files

Five recordings were captured from a real car interior and stored in `real_noises/`:

## Integration into the Playground

The real audio files were added as a new "Real Car Recording" driving scenario in the playground. When selected:
- A dropdown lets the user pick which recording (Real Car 1-5)
- The duration slider automatically adjusts to the recording's length
- The audio file is loaded and used directly as the noise source signal, replacing synthetic noise generation
- All room acoustics (RIRs, acoustic paths) still apply normally — each microphone "hears" the real audio filtered through its unique acoustic path

| Recording | Best dB | Filter | Step Size | Conv Time |

| Real Car 5 | **0.78 dB** | 512 | 0.005 | 0.12s |

### Key Observations

1. **Longer filters always win**: 512 taps is optimal for every recording. More taps = better reduction, monotonically.
2. **Small step sizes are best**: 0.001-0.005 dominates. Larger steps (0.01+) degrade or diverge.
3. **Large variance across recordings**: Real Car 4 achieves 4.04 dB (likely has more low-frequency tonal content), while Real Car 5 barely reaches 0.78 dB. The noise characteristics matter more than tuning.
4. **Short filters + aggressive steps diverge**: 64 taps at step=0.015 amplifies noise on every recording.
5. **No single optimal step size**: Ranges from 0.001 (Car 1) to 0.005 (Cars 3, 5). The optimal step depends on the noise spectrum and stationarity of each recording.
6. **Achievable range**: 0.78–4.04 dB with single-channel FxNLMS, depending on noise characteristics.

Key takeaway: Tuning filter length and step size yields modest improvement (1.8 → 2.1 dB on Car 1), but the fundamental limitation is not the filter parameters — it is the nature of real broadband noise.

## Why Real Audio is Harder

### 1. Broadband Spectrum
Synthetic scenarios generate noise with known spectral composition (engine harmonics, filtered road noise, shaped wind noise). Real car noise is fully broadband — energy spread across all frequencies, with no dominant tonal components for the filter to lock onto.

### 2. Non-Stationary Signal
Real car noise changes continuously: engine RPM fluctuations, road surface changes, wind gusts, vibrations. The adaptive filter constantly chases a moving target. By the time weights converge for one noise profile, the noise has already changed.

### 3. Frequency-Dependent Amplification (Waterbed Effect)
The frequency spectrum analysis revealed that while ANC reduces noise at some frequencies, it **amplifies** noise at others. This is the Bode sensitivity integral (waterbed effect) — suppressing energy at certain frequencies mathematically forces it to increase at neighboring frequencies. The overall 1.8 dB means cancellation barely outweighs amplification.

### 4. ANC Frequency Limits
Active noise cancellation is physically limited to low frequencies (~20-300 Hz):
- **Below 20 Hz**: Speakers can't reproduce, minimal cabin energy
- **Above 300-500 Hz**: Wavelengths become too short for spatial cancellation. A small phase error flips cancellation to amplification. The quiet zone shrinks to centimeters.
- The filter wastes capacity trying to model frequencies it cannot physically cancel

### 5. Secondary Path Modeling Error
The 5% estimation error on the secondary path (speaker → error mic) causes phase errors at certain frequencies, leading to constructive instead of destructive interference.

## Improvement Experiments

Three high-impact improvements were tested one at a time against the baseline (Real Car 1, Sedan, 30s, 256 taps, step 0.005 → 1.8 dB).

### 1. Bandpass Pre-filtering (20-300 Hz)

**Hypothesis**: Focus the adaptive filter on the ANC-effective frequency range. The filter wastes taps modeling out-of-band content it can never cancel.

**Implementation**: IIR bandpass filter (Butterworth, SOS form for numerical stability) applied to the reference signal before the adaptive filter. Configurable low/high cutoff in the sidebar.

**Results**: No measurable improvement in overall dB reduction. The bandpass correctly focuses the filter on low frequencies, but the error signal still contains out-of-band energy. Since the dB metric is computed on the full-band error, out-of-band energy dominates the metric even when in-band cancellation improves. The filter itself works — the adaptive weights converge faster and more cleanly within the passband — but the metric doesn't capture the in-band improvement.

**Verdict**: Implemented and available as a UI option, but does not improve the headline metric. Would need a frequency-weighted evaluation metric to properly assess its benefit.

### 2. Subband FxLMS

**Hypothesis**: Split the signal into K frequency bands with independent adaptive filters per band. Each sub-filter adapts optimally for its frequency range. This is how commercial ANC systems handle broadband noise.

**Implementation**: `SubbandFxLMS` class in `src/core/subband_fxlms.py`. K analysis bandpass filters split the reference, K independent FxNLMS instances (each with `filter_length // K` taps), anti-noise summed across bands, shared fullband error signal.

**Results** (Real Car 1, 30s, 512 taps):

| Bands | Step Size | Reduction | Notes |
|-------|-----------|-----------|-------|
| 4 | 0.005 | Diverged | MSE → infinity |
| 6 | 0.005 | Diverged | MSE → infinity |
| 8 | 0.005 | Diverged | MSE → infinity |
| 4 | 0.001 | 1.3 dB | Worse than baseline |
| 4 | 0.0005 | 1.6 dB | Still worse |
| 4 | 0.0001 | 1.8 dB | Matches baseline, no improvement |

**Analysis**: The shared fullband error signal is the fundamental problem. Each band's filter is updated by error containing energy from ALL bands, but each filter can only generate anti-noise in its own band. At normal step sizes, this mismatch causes instability. At reduced step sizes, the filters barely adapt. Commercial subband ANC systems use per-band error filtering (analysis filterbank on the error signal too), which our implementation lacks.

**Verdict**: Does not outperform baseline with shared fullband error. Architectural fix needed (per-band error filtering) to make this approach viable.

### 3. Leaky FxNLMS

**Hypothesis**: Adding weight decay (`w = (1 - mu*leak)*w + update`) prevents filter coefficient drift on non-stationary signals. Should reduce the amplification seen at certain frequencies in the spectrum plot.

**Implementation**: Added `leakage` parameter to `FxNLMS.__init__` and `update_weights`. The weight update becomes:
`w(n+1) = (1 - mu * leak) * w(n) - (mu * e(n) / norm) * xf(n)`
With leakage=0, this is identical to standard FxNLMS. UI slider in sidebar with options: Off, 0.0001, 0.0005, 0.001, 0.005, 0.01, 0.05.

**Results** (Real Car 1, Sedan, 30s):

At baseline step size (0.005, 256 taps):

| Leakage | Reduction | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| Off | 1.73 dB | Baseline |
| 0.0001 | 1.72 dB | No change |
| 0.0005 | 1.65 dB | Slightly worse |
| 0.001 | 1.66 dB | Slightly worse |
| 0.005 | 1.60 dB | Worse |
| 0.01 | 1.58 dB | Worse |
| 0.05 | 1.45 dB | Significantly worse |

At aggressive step sizes (256 taps):

| Step | Leakage | Reduction | Notes |
|------|---------|-----------|-------|
| 0.015 | Off | 0.18 dB | Nearly useless |
| 0.015 | 0.001 | 0.69 dB | Leakage helps stabilize |
| 0.02 | Off | -98.2 dB | Diverged |
| 0.02 | 0.001 | -47.9 dB | Still diverged but less |

With long filter (512 taps):

| Step | Leakage | Reduction | Notes |
|------|---------|-----------|-------|
| 0.003 | Off | 2.06 dB | Best baseline |
| 0.003 | 0.0001 | 2.05 dB | No change |
| 0.001 | Off | 2.09 dB | Best overall |
| 0.001 | 0.0001 | 2.09 dB | No change |

**Analysis**: Leakage's main effect is stabilizing overly aggressive step sizes (prevents divergence at step=0.02). But none of these aggressive+leaky configurations outperform the conservative baseline. At optimal step sizes (0.003-0.005), leakage either has no effect or slightly degrades performance by forcing the filter to forget what it learned.

**Verdict**: Does not improve noise reduction. Leakage addresses a symptom (coefficient drift) that isn't the root cause of poor performance on real audio. The real limitation is that broadband, non-stationary noise fundamentally challenges single-channel FxLMS regardless of weight decay.

## Summary

All three high-impact single-channel FxLMS improvements (bandpass pre-filter, subband FxLMS, leaky FxNLMS) failed to meaningfully improve noise reduction on real car recordings beyond the ~2 dB baseline. The fundamental bottleneck is not filter parameters or algorithm variants — it is that single-channel adaptive filtering with a fixed secondary path model is inherently limited on broadband, non-stationary noise in a reverberant environment.
